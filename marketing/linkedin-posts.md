## 1. The 15-minute email

Ask anyone who runs an inbox how long a "simple" customer email takes.

Read it. Find the price. Check the calendar. Write a polite reply. Double-check the number.

Around 15 minutes. Twenty times a day, that's five hours.

AI can now do most of that in seconds: it reads the email, finds your price, checks your calendar and writes the reply.

A person still reads it before it goes out. That part matters.

The typing goes. The judgement stays.

What's the one email your team writes over and over?

## 2. AI doesn't replace your team. It replaces the typing.

The fear I hear most: "Will AI replace my staff?"

In most small businesses, what it replaces is the worst part of their day:
• retyping an invoice into the accounts
• copying an enquiry into three systems
• digging through a policy file for one answer
• writing up the meeting nobody wants to write up

Your people keep the parts that need a person: the customer who's upset, the judgement call, the relationship.

The best results I've seen come from one rule: AI prepares, a person approves.

## 3. A quick sum worth doing

3 people.
5 hours a week each on admin a system could do.
A R25,000 salary.

3 × 5 × 4.33 weeks = 65 hours a month.
If a system takes over 60% of it, that's about 39 hours back.
At what those people really cost you (salary × 1.3 ÷ 173 hours), that's about R7,300 a month.

About R88,000 a year, on one small task.

Most owners have never put a number on it, because the cost is spread across many people and many days.

Try it with your own numbers (link in the comments). It takes ten seconds.

## 4. "Ask Sarah, she knows."

Every business has a Sarah.

She knows the leave policy, the delivery cut-off, which client gets 60-day terms. Half the office asks her something every day.

When Sarah is on leave, things slow down.

AI can now answer those questions from your own documents, and show the page the answer came from.

And when the answer isn't in the documents, a good system says so instead of guessing. That's the difference between useful and dangerous.

Sarah gets her afternoons back.

## 5. The calls you never hear about

Plumbers, dental practices, property agents, repair shops: they all lose business the same way.

The phone rings after hours, or during a busy morning. Nobody answers. The customer calls the next business on Google.

You never know it happened.

An AI call taker can answer, ask the right questions, write up the job and send it to whoever's on call, at 23:12 on a Sunday.

It hands anything complicated to a person. It never quotes a price you haven't given it.

How many calls did your business miss last week? Most owners don't know.

## 6. 12 boxes billed. 10 arrived.

A supplier invoice comes in. Someone types it into the accounts, line by line. Six minutes, maybe more.

What usually gets missed: line 9 billed 12 boxes, and only 10 were delivered.

AI can read the invoice, type it in, check every line against what was ordered and received, and flag the one that doesn't match.

A person checks that one line before anything is paid.

Faster, and fewer mistakes. Not because people are careless, but because nobody can check 14 lines with full attention 40 times a week.

## 7. When NOT to use AI

Some honesty from someone who builds these systems:

AI is the wrong tool when
• the task happens twice a month (the setup costs more than it saves)
• your information is a mess (fix that first, or you'll automate the mess)
• one mistake would be very costly and nobody can check the result
• a simple spreadsheet or form would do the job

A good first question isn't "where can we use AI?"
It's "what takes too long, and why?"

Sometimes the answer is AI. Sometimes it's a better form.

## 8. Start small. Stop any time.

The safest way to bring AI into a business, in four steps:

1. Pick one task that takes too long.
2. Measure it: how many, how long, by whom.
3. Try a small version on your real work for two to four weeks.
4. Measure again. Only build the full version if the numbers say so.

If the small version doesn't save enough time, you stop there and you've lost little.

Big "AI transformation" projects fail because they skip step 2. If you didn't measure before, you can't prove anything after.

## 9. The meeting that ends without a decision

Everyone leaves a meeting thinking someone else will send the notes.

Two days later: "Wait, who was booking the truck?"

AI can now listen to the meeting (with everyone's OK), and before you're back at your desk you have:
• what was decided
• who does what, by when
• what might go wrong

Each item links to the moment it was said, so nobody argues about what was agreed.

Small thing. Saves a surprising amount of follow-up.

## 10. Five signs a task is ready for AI

1. It happens every day or every week.
2. It's mostly reading something and typing it somewhere else.
3. The rules are clear enough that you could train a new person in a day.
4. A person can quickly check the result.
5. Your team complains about it.

Four out of five? It's probably worth a closer look.
